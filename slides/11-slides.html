Sampling Distributions and Standard Errors

  • A sampling distribution is a theoretical probability distribution of estimates obtained from taking repeated samples of size \(n\) from some population

    • A distribution of what we could have seen

  • A standard error is simply the standard deviation (\(\sigma\)) of the sampling distribution of an estimator.

    • A measure of how much our estimate could have varied.

  • Law of Large Numbers: As \(N \to \infty\) \(\bar{x} \to \mu\)

  • Central Limit Theorem: As \(N \to \infty\) \(\bar{x} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n})\)

Confidence Intervals

Confidence intervals provide a range of plausible values for our estimate

  • Three components:
    • Point Estimate (i.e. a mean, or coefficient)
    • Confidence Level (Often 95 percent by convention)
    • Margin of Error (+/- some range (typically 1.96*SE for 95 percent CI))
  • Confidence is about the interval
    • 95 percent of the intervals constructed in this manner would contain the true parameter in repeated samples.

Hypothesis Testing

  • A hypothesis test quantifies how likely it is that we would observe what we did (our test statistic), if some claim about the world were true (our hypothesis, typically a null ).

  • If our claim were true, then under this null hypothesis, our test statistic would have a distribution centered around the null value.

  • A p-value which describes the probability of observing a test statistic as extreme or more extreme in a world where our null hypothesis was true

    • If our p-value is small (\(p < 0.05\)), we reject the null hypothesis

      If our p-value is large (\(p > 0.05\)), we fail to reject the null, or retain the null hypothesis

Four Possible Outcomes of a hypothesis Test

  • False Positive: (Type I Error)
    • Rejecting a True \(H_0\).
    • \(\tau = 0\), but \(\hat{\tau}\) has a \(p<0.05\)
    • Probability=\(\alpha\)
  • True Positive: (Correct Decision)
    • Rejecting a false \(H_0\):
    • \(\tau \neq 0\), and \(\hat{\tau}\) has a \(p<0.05\)
    • Occurs with Probability = \(1-\beta\)
  • True Negative: (Correct Decision)
    • Failing to reject a True \(H_0\):
    • \(\tau = 0\), and \(\hat{\tau}\) has a \(p>0.05\)
    • Occurs with Probability = \(1-\alpha\)
  • False Negative: (Type II Error)
    • Failing to reject a false \(H_0\).
    • \(\tau \neq 0\) but \(\hat{\tau}\) has a \(p>0.05\)
    • Occurs with Probability= \(\beta\)

Statistical Power

  • Concept
  • Power
  • Comments
  • Consider two distributions of statistics under
    • a null of no effect (\(H_0\))
    • an effect of \(\tau\) (\(H_1\))
  • For a significance threshold of \(\alpha\) we would:
    • Fail to reject the null \(\beta\) (Type II Errors)
    • Correctly reject the null \(1 -\beta\) (Statistical Power)

Try changing \(\tau\) (the effect size), and se (the standard deviation of the effect)

Power is a function of:

  • Sample size (\(N\))
    • Larger samples, smaller standard errors (LLN)
  • Effect size (\(\tau\))
    • Bigger effects less overlap
  • Significance threshold (\(\alpha\))
    • Decrease Type 1 (False Positives) error leads to increased Type 2 (False Negatives)
  • The distribution of the data
    • Variance, asymptotic approximations

Paths to Statistical Inference

Whether we use math, bootstrapping, or shuffling, the goal of statistical inference is exactly the same: quantifying our uncertainty.

  • 1. Asymptotic Inference
    • We use the Central Limit Theorem and to assume a theoretical distribution (like the \(t\)-distribution).
    • Compare the observed test statistic to the assumed sampling distribution under the null.
    • Best for: Large samples with well-behaved data. Very fast.
  • 2. Bootstrapping (Using Resampling)
    • We draw new samples with replacement from our data to mimic drawing new samples from the population.
    • Compare the observed test statistic to the empirical approximation of the sampling distribution.
    • Best for: Constructing Confidence Intervals and Standard Errors without relying on strict assumptions.
  • 3. Randomization Inference (Permutation)
    • We shuffle the treatment labels without replacement to simulate a world where the treatment has no effect.
    • Centers on the null hypothesis.
    • Best for: Calculating exact \(p\)-values and testing hypotheses, especially in experiments with small samples.

Randomization Inference

The Lady Tasting Tea

  • The Claim: In the 1920s, Dr. Muriel Bristol claimed she could taste whether milk or tea was poured into her cup first.
  • The Experiment: Ronald Fisher prepared 8 cups of tea.
    • 4 had milk poured first.
    • 4 had tea poured first.
    • The cups were presented in a random, blinded order.
  • The Task: Identify the 4 cups that had milk poured first.
  • The Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\)): She has no tasting ability; she is just guessing blindly.

Permutations

  • If she is just guessing (\(H_0\) is true), her choices are completely independent of the actual cups.
  • How many possible ways can she choose 4 cups out of 8? \[ \binom{8}{4} = \frac{8!}{4!(8-4)!} = 70 \]
  • If she is guessing blindly, every one of these 70 combinations is equally likely.
  • If she gets all 4 correct, what is the probability of that happening by chance? 1/70 ≈ 0.014.
  • Because 0.014 < 0.05, we reject the null hypothesis!

Simulating the Null Distribution

We can simulate this process in R by randomly shuffling (permuting) the “Milk First” labels while holding her guesses constant, we generate a distribution of the test statistic under the null.

This is exactly what we do in randomization inference.

Code

# 1. Setup the actual truth (4 milk, 4 tea)
truth <- c(rep("Milk", 4), rep("Tea", 4))

# 2. Assume she got them all right (her guesses exactly match the truth)
lady_guesses <- c(rep("Milk", 4), rep("Tea", 4))

# 3. Create a function to simulate one random guess
simulate_guess <- function() {
  shuffled_truth <- sample(truth, replace = FALSE)
  # Calculate test statistic: number of correctly identified "Milk" cups
  correct_milk <- sum(shuffled_truth == "Milk" & lady_guesses == "Milk")
  return(correct_milk)
}

# 4. Repeat 10,000 times to build the null distribution
set.seed(1600)
null_distribution <- tibble(
  sim = 1:10000,
  correct_milk = replicate(10000, simulate_guess())
)

# 5. Plot
fig_tea <- null_distribution %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = correct_milk)) +
  geom_bar(fill = "grey60", color = "black") +
  geom_vline(xintercept = 4, color = "red", linetype = "dashed", linewidth = 1) +
  theme_minimal() +
  scale_x_continuous(breaks = 0:4) +
  labs(
    title = "Null Distribution of Correct 'Milk First' Guesses",
    subtitle = "Red dashed line indicates observed test statistic (4 correct)",
    x = "Number of Correct 'Milk First' Guesses",
    y = "Frequency"
  )

Plot

Comments

  • The bar chart represents the sampling distribution of our test statistic if the null hypothesis were true (she is randomly guessing).

  • The red line represents our observed test statistic (she guessed all 4 correctly).

  • Notice how rare it is to get 4 correct by chance! Only about 1.4% of our 10,000 simulations resulted in 4 correct guesses. This simulation matches the exact math (1/70 = 0.014), yielding a \(p\)-value of 0.014.

Putting It All Together

  • Inference is about uncertainty: We only have one sample, but we want to learn about the population.
  • Standard Errors: Tell us how much our estimate would vary if we took new samples.
  • Confidence Intervals: Give us a plausible range for the true population parameter based on our sample estimate and its standard error.
  • Hypothesis Testing & P-values: Help us quantify the evidence against a specific baseline claim (the null hypothesis \(H_0\)).
  • Power: Reminds us that sample size and effect size dictate our ability to detect the truth.

Controlling for Variables & Residualized Regression

Regression models partition the variance in the outcome into variance explained by the model and unexplained variance (residuals)

You should know:

  • The logic of residualized regression. You can isolate the effect of \(X_1\) on \(Y\) by regressing the residuals of \(Y\) (unexplained by \(X_2\)) on the residuals of \(X_1\) (unexplained by \(X_2\)).
  • Properties of Residuals: Residuals (\(\hat{\epsilon}\)) are orthogonal to (uncorrelated with) both the predictors and the predicted values.
  • Why coefficients change Controlling for a new variable only changes the coefficient of an existing predictor if the new variable is correlated with both the predictor and the outcome.

Difference-in-Differences (DiD)

Diff in Diff estimates causal effects by comparing the change in outcomes over time between a treated group and a control group

You should know:

  • Average Treatment on Treated (ATT): The specific causal quantity estimated (\(E[Y_{1i}(1) - Y_{0i}(1)|D=1]\)).

Estimating Uncertainty: Simulation vs. Analytic

Bootstrapping can be used to obtain a standard error and confidence intervals using resampling.

To make inferences about a population, we need to understand the sampling distribution and Standard Error (SE). There are two main ways to do this:

  • Simulation (Bootstrapping): Treat your observed sample as if it were the true population. Draw repeated samples with replacement to create a “resampling distribution.”

    • Calculating SE: The Standard Error is the standard deviation of this simulated resampling distribution (the “plug-in principle”).

  • Analytic (Asymptotic): Relies on statistical theory (Law of Large Numbers & Central Limit Theorem), which state that sampling distributions approach a normal distribution as sample size grows.
